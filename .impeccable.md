## Design Context

### Users
Primary users are landscapers and landscaping teams, including owners, managers, office staff, and crew-facing users who may not be experienced with software. They use the product in the middle of real operational work: scheduling jobs, tracking crews, managing clients, creating estimates and invoices, and keeping the day moving without confusion or delay.

The interface should reduce friction for users who are busy, mobile, and task-focused. It should favor fast recognition over interpretation, clear next steps over exploration, and obvious workflows over feature density.

### Brand Personality
The product should feel premium, intuitive, and useful.

Its tone should be clear, grounded, and professional without sounding corporate or overly technical. Users should feel confident that the product helps them stay organized and in control, not that it is demanding extra mental work from them.

The product should also feel minimal and disciplined. Copy should be sparse, direct, and only present when it materially helps the task.

### Aesthetic Direction
The visual direction should be clean, structured, and calm. The existing forest-green operational palette is a strong fit and should be used in a restrained, purposeful way rather than as decoration.

The product should not resemble dense enterprise tools like Salesforce or Meta Business Suite, where the screen feels crowded, noisy, and difficult to parse. Avoid visual clutter, too many competing actions, overly busy dashboards, and layouts that make users hunt for the next step.

Support both light and dark mode, but prioritize clarity and legibility over stylistic effects.

Favor minimal copy by default. Avoid explanatory paragraphs, duplicate labels, helper text, and secondary descriptions unless they are necessary for clarity, compliance, or error prevention.

### Design Principles
1. Make the next action obvious within a few seconds.
2. Keep screens calm and uncluttered; remove anything that does not help the task at hand.
3. Use as little text as possible; every word must earn its place.
4. Prefer plain language and familiar labels over internal or technical terminology.
5. Design for busy, non-technical users working in operational contexts, often on mobile.
6. Use premium polish through restraint, spacing, hierarchy, and consistency rather than visual excess.
